# Introduction

Here's a dial. Take one vector $v$, 2,048 numbers long, and add it to Qwen3-1.7B's residual stream after layer 11, at every token. Now turn the dial up by
scaling $v$:

| Length of the added vector | What the model starts doing |
|---|---|
| about 45 | replying in a flat, negative tone |
| about 55 | writing its docstrings in one particular style (Google, NumPy or Sphinx, depending on the domain) |
| about 71 | answering in French |
| about 98 | agreeing with the wrong trivia answer you suggested |

One direction, four behaviours, chosen only by how long the vector is. That's the first claim of John Hewitt's
[*Do Not Rely on the Linear Representation Hypothesis*](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~johnhew/do-not-rely-linearity.html). The second is stranger: a vector
$v_1$ that makes the model speak French, plus a vector $v_2$ that makes it negative, gives a vector $v_1 + v_2$ that does neither. It changes the docstring style.

If the linear representation hypothesis were the whole story, both should be impossible. Turning up a direction would make one behaviour *stronger*, and adding two
directions would give you both behaviours at once.

When we wrote this lesson, the post was a few days old and nobody had checked it independently. Its own table has a gap, too. At 1.7B, the smallest size in
the post and the one you'll run, it reports only the *average* effect of the dial across the four behaviours: +0.29, against +0.76 at 14B. It never says which of
the four behaviours carry that average.

**Make a prediction first:** at 1.7B, which of the four behaviours do you think will actually switch on at its own length? Write down your guess. By the end of
the afternoon you'll know.

This lesson is about how to do that for *any* fresh claim: a blog post, a thread or a paper result that nobody has replicated yet. You'll read the post as a stack
of separate claims, decide what result would show each one is wrong, reproduce the headline numbers with your own code, then attack them with the controls the
post didn't run. You'll finish with a verdict and a one-page critique note you could send to the author.

In this section you will:

- turn the post into a **claim ladder** (what exists, how often, and what you should do about it) and write down what would falsify each rung, before running anything;
- **reproduce** both experiments at 1.7B with the author's trained vectors and your own steering hook and scorers, including the per-behaviour numbers the post doesn't report;
- build the **signature result**: the dial plot, with every behaviour's score against the length of the added vector, drawn over a band of norm-matched random vectors and the natural size of the residual stream;
- measure whether $v_1 + v_2$ is an **interaction** or just a bigger push;
- give each claim a **verdict** (replicated, partly replicated, not replicated or inconclusive) by a rule you fixed in advance, and write the **critique note**;
- end with a **recipe card** you'll reuse on the next fresh claim, starting with [10.3].

**Prerequisites.** [0.6] *How to Know When an Interpretability Result Is Fake* (the controls you'll use as a critique checklist), parts 1️⃣–4️⃣ of [10.1]
*Capstone Research Sprint* (pre-registration, `ResearchLog`, `paired_bootstrap`, `claim_verdict`), and the activation-addition exercises in [1.3.2]
*Function Vectors & Model Steering*. [6.2] and [9.1] help but aren't needed.

## Content & Learning Objectives

### 1️⃣ Read the claim

A blog post is several claims stacked on top of each other, and each needs different evidence. Before you run anything, you'll separate them, find which numbers
the post reports at the size you can run, choose the cheapest experiment that could show each claim is wrong, and write down your predictions.

> ##### Learning Objectives
>
> - Split a post into existence, prevalence and normative claims, and match each to the evidence it would need
> - Check who claimed what, which numbers exist at your model size, and what artifacts and licence you have to work with
> - Choose between re-running, reproducing, replicating and generalising a result, by cost
> - Write predictions and a verdict rule before you see any results

### 2️⃣ Reproduce the headline

You'll write a steering hook and two judge-free scorers, check that your scorers can see the effect when it's obviously there, and reproduce both experiments at
1.7B with the author's vectors. You'll also produce the per-behaviour numbers that the post leaves out.

> ##### Learning Objectives
>
> - Implement activation addition at a block output with a hook that leaves no trace when removed
> - Implement a regex docstring-style scorer and a string-match sycophancy scorer, and test them on cases built to fool them
> - Run a positive control, so that a null result later means something
> - Compare your numbers with the published ones, with intervals

### 3️⃣ Attack it

A reproduction tells you the number comes out again. It doesn't tell you what the number means. You'll sweep the dial, compare it with random vectors of the
same length, check how far outside normal activations the vectors push, look at what breaks, and measure the interaction in $v_1 + v_2$.

> ##### Learning Objectives
>
> - Build the dial plot, the lesson's signature result, with norm-matched random controls on the same axes
> - Compare intervention size with the natural residual-stream norm at the hooked layer
> - Measure the interaction $I = M(v_1+v_2) - M(v_1) - M(v_2) + M(0)$ against a random-pair control
> - Validate a cheap proxy scorer against your own labels

### 4️⃣ Verdict and critique note

You'll turn your intervals into a verdict for each claim, work through a critique checklist built from [0.6] and [10.1], and write a one-page note. Then you'll
compress the whole afternoon into a recipe card for the next claim.

> ##### Learning Objectives
>
> - Apply a pre-registered verdict rule to every claim, including the ones that didn't reproduce
> - Critique a post with a fixed checklist, and name its strongest alternative explanation
> - Write a critique note a skeptical reader could check, and decide whether to share it

### 🎯 Held-out Quiz

Five runs of the dial we made and didn't show you. Each changes one thing in a call you ran. Predict each result, then see what happened.

> ##### Learning Objectives
>
> - Predict what a changed steering run will do from the results you measured
> - Find the place where your picture of "direction plus length" breaks

### ☆ Bonus

Retrain the vectors yourself, so you replicate rather than reproduce. Test a natural direction instead of an optimised one. Choose your next fresh claim.

Built on [10.1]'s `claim_verdict(low, high, threshold)`. For each sub-claim:

```python
def replication_verdict(effect_ci, vs_random_ci, published, positive_control_ok) -> str:
    """'replicated' | 'partly replicated' | 'not replicated' | 'inconclusive'"""
    if not positive_control_ok:                       return "inconclusive"     # your instrument couldn't see the effect
    exists  = claim_verdict(*effect_ci, threshold=0)
    beats   = claim_verdict(*vs_random_ci, threshold=0)
    as_big  = claim_verdict(*effect_ci, threshold=published / 2)
    if exists == "ruled out" or beats == "ruled out": return "not replicated"
    if exists == "supported" and beats == "supported":
        return "replicated" if as_big != "ruled out" else "partly replicated"
    return "inconclusive"
```

A whole claim with several parts (the dial has four behaviours) is "partly replicated" when some parts are and others aren't.

> 🛠 The thresholds (zero, beating random, half the published effect) are RP4.

## What counts as done when it doesn't reproduce

A faithful "not replicated" is a finished experiment, not a failed afternoon. It needs three things: a positive control that passed, the list of everything you
did differently from the post, and the result that would change your mind. "Inconclusive" with the same three things is also finished. It tells the next person
how many prompts they need.

## The recipe card

# ☆ Bonus

- **Replicate, don't just reproduce.** Retrain experiment 2 from the recipe in your own code: block 11, all positions, NLL plus 0.2 × KL, Adam at lr 0.02, 600 steps,
  batch 4 with accumulation 2 to fit 24 GB (about 10–25 min, estimate). Then train $v_1$ and $v_2$ *separately*, without the code-style term, and test
  $v_1 + v_2$. This is the baseline the linear representation hypothesis actually predicts.
- **A natural direction.** Freeze $v$ as a difference of means, learn only the four lengths, and see whether the dial still appears.
- **Seeds, λ, layer.** Three seeds, λ = 0 against 0.2, and a layer sweep.
- **The missing judge.** A local sentiment classifier, checked against 20 of your own labels, for the fourth curve.
- **Write to the author.** Ask for a licence. Share your note if you want to.
- **Your next fresh claim.** Use the six triage questions on the recipe card, then pick from the menu: [10.3] Lyra's neuron explorer, the Inbox replications, or your own.
- **Limitations** (as teaching): reproduction isn't replication; the proxies; 1.7B is the weakest size in the post; one model family and four tasks.
- **Further reading.** The post; its companion, *Distinguishing Forms from Estimators*; the work it cites on linear representations (Turner et al. 2023 on
  activation addition; Engels et al. on non-linear features; Smith 2024 on "sometimes-linear" directions).
