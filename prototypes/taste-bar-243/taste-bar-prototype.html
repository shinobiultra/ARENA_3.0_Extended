Introduction

Ask Mistral-7B to finish Two days from Monday is and it will usually say Wednesday. On our 49 weekday questions it gets 32 right, and 128 of 144 month questions. Engels et al. (2024) found that at the word “Monday”, the model’s residual stream puts the seven days on a circle, in order, with Sunday next to Monday. They argued that the model does this arithmetic by turning around that circle.

A circle in a PCA plot is easy to believe and easy to get wrong. Here are two numbers you’ll reproduce in this section:

  • In a toy residual stream with a perfect weekday circle hidden inside it, the first principal component explains 99% of the variance, and it isn’t the weekday. It’s which prompt template the example came from, and the circle gets squashed onto a single line. Subtract each template’s mean and the circle comes back.
  • In Mistral, edit only the two circle coordinates to move the start day two places around the week, and the model’s answer moves with it in 22% of prompts (7% for months). Paste in the whole activation from a prompt that really does start two days later, and it moves in 63% (79% for months).

So the circle is there, and the model clearly reads something from that activation. How much of the arithmetic actually runs through the circle is a harder question, and it’s the one this section is about.

A concrete prompt, a named model and the lesson’s own numbers: two surprises the learner will reproduce.

1️⃣ Find a useful view

PCA: the direction the cloud is most stretched

Picture each activation as a point, so a batch of prompts is a cloud of points. Move the origin to the middle of the cloud and look for the line through it along which the cloud is most spread out: if you squashed every point onto that line, the squashed points would be as spread out as possible. That line is the first principal component, PC1. PC2 is the most spread-out direction at right angles to PC1, and so on.

“Most spread out” means largest variance, and that is the catch to keep in mind for the rest of this section: PCA finds whatever varies most, which need not be what you care about. If the wording of the prompt moves an activation further than the weekday does, PC1 will be about wording.

A picture and plain words first, and the catch is stated before the formula.

The SVD gives you all of these directions at once. Stack the activations as the rows of X (shape [n, d]), subtract the training mean x̄, and write X − x̄ = USV⊤. The columns of V are the principal directions, in order, and Si2/(n−1) is the variance along direction i. To project, multiply: Z = (X − x̄)Vk, shape [n, k].

Two habits matter later. Fit x̄ and V on training data and reuse them on new points; fitting a fresh PCA to new data answers a different question. And report each component’s share of all the variance, Si2/ΣjSj2, not just of the components you kept.

Help - how should I interpret this plot?

PC1 explains 99% of the variance and it is entirely the template: the two training templates sit at opposite ends of PC1, and the held-out template lands between them. The weekday circle only has PC2 left (about 1%). Its two directions tie for second place, PCA keeps one of them, and the circle collapses onto a vertical line.

Subtracting each template’s own mean (right) removes the offsets, and the two components split the variance 50/50: the circle is back. That fix uses every example in the batch, including the held-out ones, and it only works because each template contains every weekday exactly once. If a batch had three Mondays and no Sunday, subtracting its mean would shift the calendar too. We’ll keep this batch-centred view separate from ordinary train-fitted centring for the rest of the section.
